About the Project

Sanskrit (Samskrita Bhasha) is one of the most carefully described languages in history. Its grammar was codified more than two thousand years ago, which makes it a fascinating subject for computer science.

Sanskrit and NLP

Natural Language Processing (NLP) teaches computers to read, interpret and generate human language. Sanskrit is both easy and hard for NLP: its rules are precise, but free word order, rich inflection, Sandhi and compounds make automatic analysis difficult. Research has moved from hand-written rules, to database-driven analyzers, to neural networks guided by traditional grammar.

Paninian Grammar

Panini's Ashtadhyayi (around the 4th century BCE) describes how Sanskrit words are built from roots using roughly 4,000 compact rules. Its rule-based, almost algorithmic style is why it is often compared with formal grammars and programming-language specifications. Its Karaka theory links a noun's case ending to its role in the action (agent, object, instrument and so on), which is the basis of dependency-style parsing.

Sandhi (सन्धि)

Sandhi is the joining of sounds at word boundaries. Words are written as they are spoken, so boundaries disappear. For example:

विद्या + आलयः = विद्यालयः

सूर्य + उदयः = सूर्योदयः

The reverse task, Sandhi-viccheda (splitting), is the first step for almost every Sanskrit NLP tool, because nothing else works until the words are separated.

Samasa (समास)

Samasa is compounding: two or more words merge into one, with the relationship between them left unstated. Sanskrit compounds can be very long. For example:

राज्ञः पुरुषः = राजपुरुषः

नीलं च तत् कमलम् = नीलकमलम्

A program must work out the hidden relationship, which is why compound analysis is a major research topic.

Literature reviewed in this project

  • Bharati, Chaitanya & Sangal (1995) – Natural Language Processing: A Paninian Perspective. Karaka-based parsing for free word order.
  • Huet (2003, 2009) – Sanskrit Heritage Engine. Finite-state morphological analysis and Sandhi splitting.
  • Jha et al. (2006, 2008) – JNU inflectional morphology analyzer for Subanta and Tinanta forms.
  • Sandhan (2023) – Linguistically-informed neural architectures for Sanskrit (SanskritShala).

How This Translator Works & Its Limitations

Online machine translation mode

Your sentence is sent directly from your browser to the public MyMemory translation service (api.mymemory.translated.net), which supports Sanskrit (code sa) and needs no secret key. This site has no server of its own.

  • Output is machine-generated and can contain errors in grammar, Sandhi and word choice. Treat it as a draft and check it with a Sanskrit teacher.
  • Short, common sentences work best. Idioms and long sentences may be poor.
  • Free usage is limited to about 5,000 characters per day per internet connection, and long text is sent in small pieces.
  • An internet connection is required, and your text is sent to the MyMemory service. Do not enter private information.

Basic dictionary demo mode

This mode runs entirely in your browser with a small built-in dictionary of common words and a few ready-made sentences. It is not a real translator.

  • Unknown words appear in [square brackets].
  • Words are replaced one by one, so Sanskrit case endings, verb agreement, Sandhi and word order are not handled.
  • It exists to demonstrate the idea of dictionary lookup, which is where early Sanskrit NLP tools began.
